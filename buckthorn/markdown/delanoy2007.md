(Rhamnus cathartica L.) in Saskatchewan

Abstract
Introduced to Saskatchewan in the 1930s as a
potential shelterbelt species, European buckthorn is now a
prominent understory shrub in riparian woodland and shrub
communities around Saskatoon. Locally, the Meewasin
Valley Authority (MVA) is actively controlling buckthorn
as part of its mandate to conserve natural heritage resources
of the South Saskatchewan River Valley, with the goal of
restoring the natural biodiversity of remnant patches of
native vegetation. European buckthorn is normally dioe-
cious, and MVA has chosen to treat only fruiting stems in
an attempt to limit seed production. Two control tech-
niques have been used. In one treatment, glyphosate was
applied to stems after cutting; alternatively Garlon 4 Dow
AgroSciences herbicide (active ingredient triclopyr) was
applied as a chemical girdle directly to the stems using a
streamline basal bark spray method. To date, more than
347,000 fruiting stems of buckthorn have been treated.
Results indicate good initial progress in limiting seed
production in dense buckthorn sites, but at a high cost.
Although seed eradication is not a practical short-term goal
for the Saskatoon buckthorn population, chemical girdling
can substantially and strategically reduce seed and effec-
tively limit spread. Field-tested strategies to reduce costs
and improve efﬁciencies are discussed.

Introduction
European buckthorn (Rhamnus cathartica L.), a species
native to Europe and Asia (Godwin 1943, Polunin 1969),
has become naturalized in many parts of eastern and central
North America. Like many non-native plants, it has become
invasive and threatens species-rich native ecosystems. Its
ability to dominate native ﬂora results from high individual
and population growth rates. In eastern North America, it
grows well in the light shade of deciduous woodlands. It is
especially common in stands of oak, and often establishes in
deadfall openings; fence rows, roadsides, ravines, and river
banks also provide suitable habitat (Maw 1984). In New
England, characteristic habitats include abandoned ﬁelds,
early successional forests, disturbed forest edges, ﬂoodplain
forests, open disturbed areas, pastures, planted forest,
roadsides, and vacant lots (IPANE 2001). In the Great
Plains, it readily invades riparian and upland forests and
native shrub communities. With invasion comes displace-
ment of native species, changes in community structure and
function, and a loss in biodiversity. In some jurisdictions,
buckthorn is declared a noxious weed, which carries a legal
requirement to remove it.
The adult plants have large, shading leaves that modify
understory microhabitats, and once established, outcom-
pete local species. Buckthorn is a deciduous, profusely
branched shrub or small tree that typically grows to heights
of 2–8 m. Its widespread occurrence in North America is
attributed to the similarity between naturalized and indig-
enous habitats. The species is dioecious, with male and
female ﬂowers borne on separate plants. Fruit production
occurs at a young age and is proliﬁc (Godwin 1943). The
fruits contain three to four seeds. Seeds are mainly dis-
persed by birds, but premature dispersal is minimized by
the presence of anthraquinones in the immature fruit (Trail

Degradation of natural areas due to the establishment of
dense, even-aged stands of buckthorn is of primary concern
to conservation authorities (USGS 2000). Buckthorn leafs

shading out smaller plants. For example, in Wisconsin it

buckthorn may be allelopathic (Boudreau and Willson

In Saskatchewan, buckthorn is found in two principal
areas. It has been reported in the Souris River Valley in the
southeast of the province where it is contiguous with the
U.S. Midwestern population centered in Minnesota. In
addition, buckthorn has established a disjunct population in
the South Saskatchewan River Valley at Saskatoon where
it thrives in riparian woodlands, shrub stands and aspen
groves. Buckthorn was introduced to Saskatoon as a po-
tential shelterbelt species in the 1930s at the federal gov-
ernment Dominion Tree Nursery. However, it was never
ofﬁcially released because it is an alternate host for oat
crown rust (Puccinia coronata var. avenae W.P. Fraser
Ledingham), a virulent disease that affects grain yield and
quality (Harder and Chong 1983). Since this time, buck-
thorn has spread along the river valley up to 56 km
northeast and 21 km southwest from the original site of
introduction (Figure 1).
The combination of heavy fruit production and high
germination rates allows buckthorn to rapidly dominate an
understory, and its proliﬁc nature makes it difﬁcult to
eradicate. Various methods can be used, and repeated
treatments may be required to kill mature plants and
eliminate the seed bank. Fire is effective in killing buck-
thorn seedlings and can top-kill mature shrubs, but the
species resprouts vigorously and burning usually needs to
be repeated annually or biennially over several years before
it is eradicated (Heidorn 1991). Similarly, cutting or gir-
dling typically results in new shoot development from the
stumps (Heidorn 1991). Chemical control is the most
common method of managing buckthorn, but like the other
methods, repeated treatment is required for complete
eradication. Stump applications of glyphosate in the fall, or
wick applications in the spring have been used effectively
in some jurisdictions. Garlon 4 has also been successful
when sprayed immediately on to cut stumps during the
growing season (Converse 1984). In addition, some work
has been conducted on biological control, but no methods
have been approved to date (Gassman and others 2002).
Locally, the Meewasin Valley Authority (MVA) is ac-
tively controlling buckthorn as part of its mandate to con-
serve natural heritage resources of the South Saskatchewan
River Valley in Saskatoon. The goal of MVA is to restore the
natural biodiversity of remnant patches of native vegetation.
To date, more than 347,000 fruiting stems of buckthorn have
been treated with herbicide. This paper describes the prac-
tices, costs, and success of the program initiated in 1998 to
control the spread and impact of buckthorn. The results of
this work have been applied to the entire valley population,
and the feasibility of seed and stem control as well as native
plant restoration are discussed.

Study Area
The general extent of buckthorn distribution around Sas-
katoon (52 N 106 W) is illustrated in Figure 1. The map
was compiled from ﬁeld surveys conducted in late Sep-
tember to mid-October from 1998 through 2005 at a time
when the green foliage of buckthorn is readily distin-
guished from the native species. Most surveys were com-
pleted by boat, supplemented with vehicle traverses along
the grid roads, and by on-site stem counts carried out
during subsequent herbicide treatments. Information was
recorded in the ﬁeld on 1:10,000 color air photos. The inset
photo (Figure 1) is a typical ﬁeld map and illustrates the
complex pattern of patch shape, size, and density of the
buckthorn stands. The map presents aggregate data for one-
quarter-section land units derived from the township and
range survey grid.
Buckthorn is more widely distributed northeast of Sas-
katoon, and density decreases with distance from the city
and the river. The valley of the South Saskatchewan River
is characterized by a complex of landforms. North of
Saskatoon, the valley is more incised and bounded by rock-
laden moraine typiﬁed by clay-based soils. To the south, a
wind-formed dune complex dominates the upland. This
aeolian landscape is characterized by rough hummocky to
undulating topography. Here the thin topsoils are formed
from ﬁne to medium sands. These sandhills conﬁne a wide,
meandering, poorly drained ﬂoodplain. Thus, buckthorn is
progressively restricted to coulees and similar valley side
features typically where colluvial deposits provide moist,
yet well-drained conditions.
Methods
Buckthorn was recorded at 239 land parcels, of which 118
have received herbicide treatment; of these, 85 have been
treated once and 33 twice. In this study, only fruiting stems
were treated. Stands that were sprayed twice received the
second herbicide application about 5 years after the ﬁrst
treatment.
Sutherland Beach, an area of river terraces located
within the city limits of Saskatoon (Figure 1) has a large
stand of buckthorn, part of which remains untreated and
part of which has received two treatments. In the treated
section, fruiting stems were cut and sprayed in 1998 and
sprayed again in 2004. Sutherland Beach supports some of
the densest buckthorn stands in the area and provides a
good baseline for estimating the effectiveness of treatment
strategies. In 2005, three 5-m · 5-m plots were established
in both the treated and untreated parts of the site for the
purpose of quantifying stand structure, fruit production,
and seed reserves.
Stand structure was assessed by measuring basal diam-
eters of all large stems—basal diameters of 1 cm or lar-
ger—and by distinguishing them as fruiting (female) or
nonfruiting (male). In addition, in each plot four subplots
measuring 50 cm · 50 cm were randomly established for
the purpose of estimating groundlayer density. All stems
with basal diameters measuring less than 1 cm were
counted.
Total fruit loads were estimated in late fall, 2005 and
this late timing necessitated both on-stem and on-ground
fruit counts. Stem fruit counts and basal diameters of all
fruiting stems were recorded in the plots. Fallen fruit
counts were made in three 50-cm · 50-cm subplots ran-
domly selected in each 5-m · 5-m plot. Fallen leaves and
other debris were carefully removed prior to on-ground
fruit counts. Soil samples to a depth of 10 cm and totaling
0.3 m2 were taken from the 50-cm · 50-cm subplots to
establish the magnitude of buried seed reserves. The soil
samples were randomly selected within each subplot,
aggregated and screened with a 2-mm sieve. All seeds were
opened to determine whether the endosperm was intact.
Two control techniques were used during the course of
the project. During the ﬁrst 2 years of the project (1998 and
1999), a stump/glyphosate spray method was used. With
this method, large stems were cut with a chainsaw and
smaller stems either were cut with a pruning saw or with
branch loppers. The cut stump surface was sprayed with a
50% solution of glyphosate in the fall season. Freezing
weather applications were avoided because glyphosate was
mixed with water. Both glyphosate and Garlon 4 herbicides
were applied according to the procedures speciﬁed by the
manufacturers using a small 1.9-L polyethylene hand-
pressurized
sprayer
and
with
appropriate

equipment. In the second treatment (2004), Garlon 4 was
applied using a streamline basal bark spray method with a
30% solution as recommended for dormant season appli-
cation. This has been the method of choice since 2000
because of its great savings in labor. Another advantage in
using Garlon 4 is that it can be mixed with a light mineral
oil (Isopar M, Exxon Mobil Corporation) and applied at
temperatures between 25C and –15C. This can extend the
work season as well as eliminate the ‘‘brown-out’’ phase
during which time unsightly dead leaves remain on the
shrubs.
The amount of chemical used was tracked for Garlon 4
but not for glyphosate; 495 L of 30% solution were used to
treat 283,362 stems. On average, about 1900 stems were
treated per undiluted liter of Garlon 4, which provided an
estimate of the volume of glyphosate needed for stump
treatments. Based on the average basal diameter of buck-
thorn stems measuring 1 cm or larger in three untreated 25
m2 plots at Sutherland Beach, the average surface area of a
stump cut is 8 cm2 versus 5 cm2 for basal bark applications.

Results
Treatment Effects
The plot data indicate that treatments of both stump/
glyphosate and basal bark/Garlon 4 result in very high kill
rates. Plants that survive these treatments generally exhibit
malformed regrowth or partial topkill; typically this was not
observed. The chance of survival increases when Garlon 4 is
tainted with water during basal bark application. When
Garlon 4 is applied to a wet target, the emulsiﬁer contained
within it will be triggered and the spray solution will form an
emulsion. This tainted condition is identiﬁed as a white,
milky solution that will run quickly down the stem. This will
prevent movement of the active ingredient into the bark,
resulting in complete loss of efﬁcacy (Dow Agrosciences
1999). This was a particular problem when the chemical was
applied on the colder days when the stems were covered
with frost. The problem of water contamination was rec-
ognized as an application issue early in the study in some of
the initial test areas, and in subsequent trials incomplete kill
was attributed to female plants that had escaped treatment.
Typically, plants were missed because of problems of
identiﬁcation in denser stands. Lower error rates were noted
for initial treatments in well-established thickets because the
shrubs were quite large and comparatively widely spaced. In
follow-up treatments not only were the shrubs smaller, but
some were partially obscured by deadfall. Detection of
stems varied with light and weather conditions, and was
especially difﬁcult in the early morning and late afternoon or
when there was heavy cloud cover.
Miss rates were particularly high for stump/glyphosate
treatments when cutting and spraying were not combined
in one operation, because of the difﬁculty in relocating
stumps. Even with the use of ﬂagging, 21% of the stumps
were not sprayed when treatment was carried out in a two-
step operation over the course of several hours. This was
reduced to 6% after a second treatment 6 years later.
Recruitment, mainly from buried seed reserves, continued
to generate new seedlings; however, the number of fruiting
stems (those escaping treatment in 1998 and those that
developed after the 1998 treatment) was reduced to 26% of
the initial population. The rate reduction in fruiting stems is
projected to accelerate with each additional treatment. This
trend should continue to increase because the cumbersome
two-step operation responsible for higher escape rates was
discontinued.
Stand Structure
In the undisturbed buckthorn thickets at Sutherland Beach,
there is a two-layered understory beneath the open canopy
of green ash (Fraxinus pennsylvanica Marsh.) and Mani-
toba maple (Acer negundo L. var.interius (Britt.) Sarg.).
The dense taller buckthorn layer, 2–5 m high, is composed
of well-spaced mature male and female shrubs. The shorter
layer consists of a ground cover dominated by buckthorn
seedlings and stunted saplings reaching 50 cm in height. A
total of 71 nonfruiting and 72 fruiting stems measuring 1
cm or greater basal diameter was recorded in the untreated
plots, equivalent to a density of 9467 ha–1 and 9,600 ha–1,
respectively. In the treated plots, from which the fruiting
stems had been culled, the stands were highly skewed to-
ward a nonfruiting population with a ratio of 135 to 22.
This is an expected and positive outcome of the treatment
program. The voids left in the shrub layer of the treated
stand were ﬁlled by a dense and vigorous growth of
buckthorn saplings with basal diameters of less than 1 cm.
This observation was supported by density measurements
showing higher counts in the understory of treated plots (98
m–2 versus 69 m–2).
Fruit Production
A chronological record was compiled of changes in fruiting
stem numbers and diameters within treated plots for the
period 1998–2005 (Figure 2). This was possible because of
the presence of stumps (glyphosate/stump treatment from
1998) and standing dead stems (basal bark treatment from
2004) at the time of sampling (2005). Between 1998 and
2004, the number of fruiting stems decreased from 82 to
74, with a corresponding change in mean basal diameter
from 3.4 to 2.1 cm. The number of fruiting stems was
further reduced to 22 after the 2004 treatment. However,
the average basal diameter increased to 2.5 cm, possibly a
result of improved light and/or other resources.

Buried Seeds
A density of 15 buckthorn seeds per square meter was
reported in 10-cm-deep soil samples collected from the
untreated site compared to 7 m–2 in the treated site; of
these, only 7 of the fruits extracted from soils in the un-
treated plot were still intact. Numerous empty choke cherry
(Prunus virginiana L.var. melanocarpa (A.Nels.) Sarg.)
pits were found throughout the samples, indicating either
that choke cherry was once more common, or that choke
cherry seeds are readily moved from distant sites. Many
choke cherry pits had an end cleanly cut off, suggesting
possible dispersal by small animals. A few green ash seeds
also were found and could be a potential natural source of
tree seedling recruitment.
Stand Diversity
The only herbaceous plant found in the understory of the
sample plots was the native Sprengel’s sedge (Carex
sprengelii Dewey). Sprengel’s sedge is listed as one of the
dominant plants typical of a rich herb layer in native shrub
stands in the region (Thompson and Hansen 2001). At the
study site, Sprengel’s sedge was sparsely distributed with
no visible seed production, a further indication that this site
is degraded. The stand supports few tree seedlings and
saplings; only buckthorn showed vigorous recruitment.
Four choke cherry seedlings were found in the treated sites
but none were present in the untreated sites, and no ash or
maple seedlings were found at either site. Choke cherry
and green ash saplings were more robust in the treated site,
where competition had been reduced, compared to the
occasional specimens found growing in association with
buckthorn elsewhere at the site.
Treatment Costs
Cost estimates were derived from counting and treating
more than 347,000 stems over an 8-year period. Estimates
were based on a wide range of ﬁeld and labor conditions.
Paid and volunteer labor was equally valued at $15 per
hour. Costs were tracked for labor and chemical but not
for other items, such as survey and mapping, land access,
administration, equipment, pesticide licensing, or vehi-
cles.
Labor inputs are far higher than chemical costs (Table 1).
The average rate of cutting and application of glyphosate in
1998 and 1999 was 19.1 and 18.9 stems per hour, respec-
tively, based on a total of 64,407 stems. This was consid-
erably lower than the average rate of treatment of 91.3 stems
per hour for basal bark/Garlon 4, which was applied to
283,362 stems. The average annual rate of treatment for
basal bark/Garlon 4 ranged from 62.0 stems hr–1 in 2001 to
254.7 stems hr–1 in 2005. This increase in efﬁciency is
attributed to two main factors: employing a smaller, more
experienced crew and treating a greater number of low-
density sites in the fall when green buckthorn plants can be
readily detected. Treating low-density sites was expensive
because most of the labor was used in traveling to and from
the sites or searching for buckthorn stems. Treatment re-
cords from particular sites indicate that as many as 375
stems can be treated per hour in very dense sites compared to
as few as 2 stems per hour in the lowest density sites.

The average cost for Garlon 4 was $31.25 per liter. The

Discussion
Results from the Sutherland Beach test plots illustrate the
high costs involved in reducing seed production in dense
stands, even using an efﬁcient chemical girdling method. It
is widely recognized that a single treatment of fruiting
stems will not provide complete seed control (Pearson and
Gillette 1999). Inevitably some fruiting stems will escape
treatment and/or some immature female stems, seedlings,
or seeds will mature in the future. Indeed, for every 100
stems treated initially, an equal additional number was
treated by completion of the third pass. Although results
are positive with a 120-fold reduction in the number of
fruits produced after two chemical treatments over a 6-year
period and with virtually no seed remaining in the soil,
costs for three treatments are in excess of $3400 ha–1 using
the Garlon 4 basal streamline application method (chemical
girdle). If this can be achieved, a long-term chemical gir-
dling program would be an efﬁcient way to treat the few
fruiting stems that do develop. However, if buckthorn re-
establishes in the ground layer, there is a long-term threat
of seed production as the young female stems mature.
Chemical girdling can play an important role in
reversing the spread of buckthorn. Its use is most effective
at the perimeter of the population where densities are low,
but there is a greater threat of expansion into new sites.
Satellite population centers might also be considered for
treatment, with established thickets being treated later in
the program. Eliminating seed production in dense thickets
is difﬁcult, although seedlings produced in these stands
have a lesser chance of establishing. Thus, it is more
advantageous to suppress small satellite colonies of inva-
sive species than the main population center because costs
are much lower on a per-area basis and vegetation response
is better with a strong shift to native species (Moody and
Mack 1988). Reduced chemical use also lessens the envi-
ronmental impact of the program. Similarly, there is less
visual impact and intrusion on wildlife habitat.
MVA has mapped the distribution of buckthorn, and this
together with knowledge of the point and time of origin
allows prediction, with some certainty, of the rate and
pattern of future spread. At this time, the Saskatoon area is
infested with a relatively youthful expanding population,
which fortunately has not established over a wide area.
Mapping has yielded information that was useful for bud-
geting and strategically implementing the control program.
Total labor and chemical costs for three chemical girdling
treatments are estimated at just under $160,000. Assuming
an equal treatment cost for each stem, 65% of the entire
area around Saskatoon can be treated for less than 1% of
the cost of the initial work, and 92% of the area can be
treated for 22% of the cost (Table 2). Buckthorn is dioe-
cious and can spread only by seed. Treating only the fe-
male plants helps to reduce initial programme costs, and
can achieve the goal of stopping the population spread by
eliminating seed production. If complete restoration of
affected areas is the goal then removal of male shrubs is
eventually required.
Autumn is an ideal time for mapping, cutting, and
spraying buckthorn. Treating low-density sites is very
efﬁcient in the fall because the foliage is highly visible and
even after the foliage has dropped, the heavy black fruit
loads remain intact for several weeks and are easily dis-
tinguished. Weather conditions also are favorable for out-
door activities, which allows the treatment program to be
extended well past the initial frosts. Garlon 4 in basal bark
applications can be sprayed to –15C, and in Saskatoon
such temperatures typically prevail through November.

A similar routine is advocated in Minnesota, where
spraying occurs from mid-October to mid-November be-
fore the snow pack is too deep (Pearson and Gillette 1999).
Another beneﬁt of fall herbicide application is that it
eliminates the unsightly retention of dead leaves known as
the ‘‘brown-out’’ phase that is characteristic of spring and
summer treatments. In addition, the impact on nesting birds
and other wildlife is reduced. One drawback to late fall
application is the need to use a higher concentration of
herbicide for dormant plants (Dow Agrosciences 1999).
Also there is the problem of stem frost which restricts
chemical penetration in basal bark applications.
Buckthorn typically requires 5–6 years before it fruits.
Rapid depletion of the seed bank means that seed longevity
is short; immediate and thorough eradication of fruiting
stems is not a pressing issue. In Saskatoon, Archibold and
others (1997) recorded a high germination rate of 88% for
freshly fallen seed with an ensuing rapid loss of viability
resulting in few germinating beyond 1 year of dispersal.
Miller (2005) similarly reported that only a small fraction
of the annual seed crop remains dormant and viable for
future years. Studies conducted in New Hampshire on
glossy buckthorn (Rhamnus frangula L.), a close relative of
the species described here, showed that removing the older
shrubs resulted in a ﬁvefold increase in seedlings the fol-
lowing year, but this was considerably reduced in the
second year (Frappier and others 2004). Establishment of
an herbaceous ground cover similarly can help to reduce
potential establishment through predation. Under herba-
ceous cover, 100% seed predation, probably by deer mice
(Peromyscus spp.), was reported by Gill and Marks (1991).
Sites with low densities often have an intact herbaceous
ground layer, yet another reason for targeting them ﬁrst.
The time for buckthorn seedlings to reach maturity was
incorporated into the operational design of the present
study. The results suggest that a 5-year treatment rotation is
appropriate to control a buckthorn population. Efﬁcacy was
increased when treatments were carried out initially in two
successive seasons in order to address the relatively high
‘‘miss’’ rate that occurs. Although the cost for chemical
girdling fruiting stems is high ($0.18), after two treatments
it appears that the test population had stabilized and a two-
layer structure characteristic of native woodland was
beginning to develop. Subsequent chemical treatments
should reduce the dominance of male buckthorn in the tall
shrub layer, and eliminate any residual seedlings and
stunted saplings present in the ground layer.
With three to ﬁve chemical girdling treatments of fru-
iting stems several positive outcomes should become evi-
dent; these include elimination of in-situ fruit production
and seed reserves in the soil, and the emergence of a stable
male-dominated tall shrub layer. Two treatments over a 6-
year period at Sutherland Beach has resulted in a strong
male-dominated composition with 135 male and 22 female
stems recorded. In the densest stands, the ground layer was
still dominated by buckthorn. However, at this stage of
treatment, the ground layer is the only potential source of
propagules because it harbors undeveloped female stems.
Long-term seed control will depend on either removing
fruiting stems as they develop from the ground layer or by
taking a more proactive approach to remove the ground
layer. Although removal can be an onerous task, it can be
implemented over a longer time-frame, ideally as part of a
larger effort designed to increase biodiversity of the local
ﬂora and fauna. It can be done as opportunities arise
through small-scale maintenance work and would allow
preferred micro-sites to be selected. In the Saskatoon re-
gion, for example, ideal sites would exhibit the following
characteristics:
1.
small numbers of buckthorn seedlings/saplings;
2.
close source of seed from native plants, such as ash
and maple trees;
3.
remote from invasive plants such as smooth brome
(Bromus inermis Leyss) and Kentucky blue grass (Poa
pratensis L.);
4.
presence of native shrubs, grasses, or herbs that are
adapted to ﬁre, such as snowberry and fescue (Festuca
sp.).
There are many ways to remove buckthorn from the
ground layer. Foliar sprays, such as glyphosate, are effec-
tive in the autumn because the chemical is readily trans-
ported into the root system. Spraying dormant small stems
with a 1% solution of Garlon 4 resulted in kill rates of 86%
(Larson 2002). An added beneﬁt of dormant season
spraying is the reduced chance of injury to herbaceous
plants (Gover and others 2002). Small-scale mechanical
control using weed trimmers to take the material to the
ground is a possibility. Similarly, mowers can be used in
grasslands and open stands where access is not a problem.
Fire is cited as an effective control of seedlings, although
saplings are more resistant. Burning is the preferred
method in communities that are adapted to ﬁre (Heidorn
1991). One difﬁculty is that buckthorn typically shades out
understory plants; this reduces leaf litter and cured fuel
loads, which can limit the spread and intensity of the ﬁre
(Gale 2001). A contained heat source, such as a propane
torch, could apply ﬁre to individual plants in the ground
layer in a reasonably efﬁcient manner. Burning is recom-
mended during the growing season in the year after the
mature plants have been killed. Buckthorn leafs out earlier
than most native species, and is top-killed by burning in
late April or early May. Carbohydrate levels are low in
roots at this time, and resprouting vigor may be reduced
(Converse 1984). However, burned areas are readily

Sheep have been used to control buckthorn in New
England (Milleken 2004) and were introduced to a site near
Saskatoon as part of the present study. Preliminary obser-
vations indicate that sheep will readily browse on taller
buckthorn but strongly avoid shorter stems in the ground
layer. Studies in Wisconsin found signiﬁcant differences in
foliar nitrogen and potassium levels in open-grown buck-
thorn compared to individuals growing in the understory of
adjacent forested sites, which could account for this
avoidance (Harrington and others 1989). In addition,
emodin, a chemical that is known to deter herbivores, is
found in buckthorn leaves and bark (Izhaki 2002). Obvious
avoidance of buckthorn bark by native wildlife was noted
at Sutherland Beach. Although buckthorn stems greatly
outnumbered native stems, they remained untouched while
32 choke cherry stems and 2 saskatoon stems had been
girdled over the winter. Emodin levels increase with light
(Van Den Berg and others 1988), although Paneitz and
Westendorf (1999) reported a continual drop from spring to
fall. Kubiak (1977) noted that emodin levels peaked at
three times during the year: April, July–August, and
November. Browsing the ground layer could be very efﬁ-
cient if sheep are introduced to the area at a time when
emodin levels are low.
Controlling the ground layer will not always result in
natural recruitment of native plants, although this may be
enhanced if all buckthorn stems are removed from an area.
Heneghan and others (2002) speculated that changes in soil
chemistry are a key factor that eliminates native ﬂora be-
neath stands of buckthorn and can effectively retard re-
establishment of native ﬂora long after buckthorn is com-
pletely eradicated. However, Boudreau and Willson (1992)
reported that autumn removal of buckthorn in Minnesota
resulted in natural establishment of 26 herb species in
riparian woodland and 6 herb species in oak savanna/rock-
outcrop prairie the following spring.

The presence of exotic species in the Saskatoon area has
been linked to changes in the disturbance regime, which
has led to other changes such as conversion of aspen groves
to tall native shrubs (Thorpe and Godwin 1992). Fire and
occasional grazing may be required to ensure the long-term
restoration success. If the goals of buckthorn control are to
improve levels of biodiversity or to construct a historically
authentic landscape, then a good understanding of original
vegetation composition is needed. A reasonable model is
available for woodland patches in the prairie region of
North America (Thompson and Hansen 2001). Ideally, and
over the long term, implementation of a control program
for buckthorn should dovetail with strategies sympathetic
with achieving high biodiversity and historical authenticity
in remnant natural woodlands.
Awareness of buckthorn and its negative impacts on
biodiversity is very low. Working with the community in a
hands-on approach is an excellent way to increase aware-
ness, and the autumn is an ideal time to coordinate with
specialized school programs. In this study, 15 students in a
local high school program participated in evaluating seed
production in treated and untreated sites. MVA also is
exploring other community-based projects to advance
awareness. Buckthorn wood is currently marketed in the
United States because of its ornamental quality with yellow
sapwood encircling rich orange heartwood; local wood-
workers represent another potential group to assist with
shrub removal. Similarly, private landowners can be en-
gaged in removal by providing them with a sound plan.
Conclusion
In the Saskatoon area, chemical girdling is an efﬁcient option
for reducing seed production except in dense thickets. Al-
though seed production has been reduced 120-fold with two
chemical treatments over a 6-year period, high numbers of
fruiting stems can raise costs to over $3400 ha–1 for three
treatments. Plants that were missed in the initial application
and recruitment plants from seed, seedlings, and saplings can
restore stand density to its original level. Signiﬁcant seed
reduction occurs after the second application of herbicide,
and if this is followed by a third treatment, complete eradi-
cation of buckthorn seed production should be possible with
occasional maintenance. The success rate at the outset of a

projected to fall dramatically. Even so, the high initial
treatment costs for dense stands warrant exploration of more

sources toward treating low-density sites ﬁrst.
Besides lower costs, there are other reasons for directing
resources toward low-density stands. Low-density sites
typically exhibit higher growth rates, and targeting them
ﬁrst is an efﬁcient way to curtail future seed production.
Furthermore, seed control there is more strategic because it
restricts seed from spreading beyond the current distribu-
tion. Vegetation response tends to be positive and returns to
native species because the understory is not yet converted
to buckthorn. Chemical use is lower on a per-areas basis,
resulting in ﬁscal and environmental advantages. Finally,
there is less visual impact and intrusion on wildlife habitat.
Mapping the buckthorn distribution yields valuable
information about stand locations, shapes, and densities. In
Saskatoon, the distribution indicates a youthful expanding
population to the north with a high number of low-density
sites available for treatment. It is estimated that 65% of the
affected area can be treated relatively cheaply. Although
low-density sites are less costly to treat on a per area basis,
they are more costly on a per-stem basis. Several strategies
can reduce these costs. Mapping and treating in the autumn
when buckthorn visibility is high offers particularly good
returns in low-density sites. Careful planning of ﬁeld trips
to reduce travel costs also is an important consideration. In
many cases where accessing or locating stems is difﬁcult
and the number of stems is small, treating both male and
female stems is advisable because this will reduce the need
for costly return trips.
The spread of many exotic species including buckthorn
can be linked to causal factors, such as changes in the
disturbance regime. Re-establishing this regime through
periodic burning or grazing is advocated as a long-term
proactive method to restore remnant natural areas in the
Saskatoon region. Reactive measures such as chemical
girdling can limit spread and buy time until a long-term
program can be established. For conservation agencies with
limited resources, it is important to maximize efﬁciencies.
Containing buckthorn seed production to a few dense
stands is a reasonable expectation. A long-term mainte-
nance program to remove fruiting stems developing outside
of these few stands can effectively control spread.
